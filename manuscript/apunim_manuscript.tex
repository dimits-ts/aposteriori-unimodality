\title[Quantifying and Auditing Group-Based Disagreement]{When Silver Isn't Gold: Quantifying and Auditing Group-Based Disagreement in Human and Persona-Driven LLM Annotation}

\author{Anonymous}


\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
	LLM annotation is increasingly used as a scalable substitute for human annotations, including in polarized, subjective tasks where humans systematically disagree along group lines. Whether these systems reproduce, ignore, or distort human disagreement patterns is largely untested, in part because most existing methods for attributing \ac{sysdis} have not yet been tested in realistic, subjective annotation settings. This work represents the first empirical study which applies proposed, theoretical methods on large scale, realistic annotation settings. Observing the weaknesses of these methods, we develop a method that not only detects, but also quantifies attribution of \ac{atsysdis} to annotator \acp{sdd} and use it to audit nine open-source LLMs against five real-life datasets on subjective \ac{nlp} tasks. Our results show that while persona-prompted LLMs can produce polarized annotations, the exhibited disagreement patterns are not aligned with those of human annotator \acp{sdd}, even when tuning instruction prompts for this purpose. This limitation indicates that applications, studies and resources using persona prompting should include appropriate scoping and evaluation in order to avoid further model miscalibration. Our study also suggests that the structure of current subjective NLP datasets is insufficient, requiring more annotators per item, as we find that not all \ac{sysdis} can be attributed to any combination of known or latent annotator \acp{sdd}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\begin{figure}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{disagreement_vs_polarization.png}
	\caption{Three kinds of disagreement in a hypothetical hate-speech annotations of a controversial comment. Each point is one annotator's rating, from $\heartsuit\heartsuit\heartsuit$ (not hateful) to \textbf{!!!} (very hateful). \emph{Left:} ratings spread uniformly across the scale, an extreme case of disagreement. \emph{Middle:} \acs{sysdis}, where a subset of annotators consistently rates the comment differently from the rest, although the cause is not visible from the ratings alone. \emph{Right:} the same ratings as the middle panel, grouped by religion. \acresetall

The rise of internet spaces has necessitated research in subjective phenomena such as the detection of harmful content. Annotations are needed to support social science research, the training of automated classifiers and the proactive identification of vulnerable groups \citep{vidgen_2021_directions}. These annotations are frequently released as datasets, which due to their subjective nature typically contain items with very low \ac{iaa} \citep{vidgen_2021_directions}. If ignored, this disagreement may lead to loss of valuable information \citep{pavlopoulos-likas-2024} and provide us with the wrong signal, leading to significant misconfiguration in trained AI systems \citep{uma2021}. For example, toxicity models have disproportionately marked \ac{aa} speech as ``toxic'' \citep{sap-etal-2019-risk}, or systematically ignored minority opinions \citep{quaranto2022dog} in the past.

Current literature lacks precise terminology to describe opinion differences between groups. We thus use the following definitions in this study:
\begin{enumerate*}
	\item \textbf{Disagreement} represents any variance among annotators' ratings for the same item, even if minute.
	\item  \textbf{\ac{sysdis}} is non-random and recurring disagreement. 
	\item \textbf{\ac{atsysdis}} is \ac{sysdis} that can be attributed to differences in group identity (e.g., background, cultural context, ideology).
	\item  \textbf{Polarization} is the mechanism with which we can distinguish \ac{sysdis} from \ac{atsysdis}.
\end{enumerate*}
In physics, a \emph{polarizer} is an optical filter with which a set of tangled rays can be untangled to ``pure'' signals. In our study, the metaphorical polarizer is each of the \acp{sdd} of an annotator group. If \ac{sysdis} (our ``beam'' in this case) is reduced when considering the groups emerging from an \ac{sdd} (e.g., left-wing/right-wing when considering political orientation), it becomes \ac{atsysdis}. Fig.~\ref{fig:disagreement-vs-polarization} demonstrates this distinction.

Researchers traditionally attempted to distinguish \emph{Disagreement} from \ac{sysdis}, but few studies moved towards discovering \ac{atsysdis}. In both cases, \ac{iaa} metrics are typically used both to distinguish between general disagreement and \ac{sysdis} and to attribute it to specific annotator groups. Traditional metrics however hold significant limitations, especially when few annotations per item are available and when the studied \acp{sdd} contain imbalanced groups. Both of these issues are typically present in subjective tasks featuring minorities, which is the exact case where our measures should be reliable. 

Furthermore, as human annotation is generally expensive, LLM-as-judge systems are increasingly deployed as scalable substitutes for human annotators \citep{rossi_2024, kapania-etal-2025-examining} to acquire a cheaper, ``silver'' supervision signal in place of human-annotated ``gold'' labels.\footnote{What constitutes ``gold'' or ``silver'' labels is not standardized and the terms frequently change definitions between publications. Some advocate against using the term ``gold labels'' for subjective, human annotations \citep{fleisig-etal-2024-perspectivist, valerio_2021_gold}.} It is therefore imperative that \ac{atsysdis} by human annotators be preserved by LLMs whose annotations by default gravitate toward the statistical average \citep{bisbee_2023}. Persona-prompting is sometimes used to bypass this limitation, but suffers from its own challenges such as stereotyping and inconsistent annotations \citep{lutz-etal-2025-prompt, liu-etal-2024-evaluating-large}. We therefore ask:

\begin{researchquestion}[How Do Current Theoretical Polarization Attribution Approaches Behave in Realistic Annotation Scenarios]
	Our work is the first empirical study applying measures of polarization detection and attribution in real-life subjective datasets, finding that they tend to severely underperform. One of the reasons for this collapse is likely the assumption that annotations can be aggregated across items (Finding~\ref{finding:methods}).
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{researchquestion}[How Can \ac{sysdis} be Quantitatively Attributed to Annotator \acp{sdd}]
	\label{rq:measure}
	Since we can not reliably used established approaches, we introduce a new measure which \emph{quantitatively} attributes \ac{sysdis} to specific \acp{sdd}, while being conscious of the limitations present in realistic annotation scenarios (Fig.~\ref{fig:measures}). 
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{researchquestion}[Do LLM Annotators Exhibit \ac{atsysdis}, and if so, Which  Patterns are Shared Between Them and Humans]
	\label{rq:llm}
	Unlike prior work which framed the question of LLM annotation reliability in terms of human-LLM agreement \citep{dong-etal-2024-llm, gomez-etal-2024-algorithmic, masud-etal-2024-hate} or task-specific performance \citep{gupta2024bias}, we compare human and LLM polarization patterns. We find that human \ac{atsysdis} is explained by gender and ethnicity across five real-life subjective datasets (Finding~\ref{finding:human}). On the other hand, while LLMs using persona-prompting \emph{can} produce polarized annotations, they fail to reproduce any \ac{atsysdis} patterns regardless of instruction prompt, confirming literature suggesting that persona prompting does not consistently influence LLM outputs in the examined tasks (Finding~\ref{finding:llms}).
\end{researchquestion}

Overall, our work indicates that \emph{persona-prompting can not be used by itself to ensure fair and equitable judgments} across \ac{sdd} groups by LLMs. Without careful scoping and task-specific evaluation, persona prompting can lead to LLMs not taking into account minority-group sensitivities, which could compromise proposed applications with autonomous agents in sensitive environments (e.g., content moderation, facilitation) and contaminate future LLM training, amplifying existing model biases, and lead to misleading findings in social science research. Our work also argues that even with recent methodological improvements (e.g., phasing out majority voting), \emph{the field of subjective \ac{nlp} has not adequately addressed the challenge of analyzing multiple perspectives} (\S\ref{sec:discussion}). Most current datasets do not hold an adequate amount of annotator \acp{sdd}, nor enough annotators per item, an issue which has not yet been discussed in literature. Indeed, we discover ``\ac{unpol}'' which can not be observed even when exhaustively considering all possible annotator dimensions in real-life datasets and is caused by low annotation counts per item (Finding~\ref{finding:inherent}).

We also release a PyPI library implementing our measure, our LLM annotated datasets totaling more than $3{,}000{,}000$ annotations, as well as the code and reproducibility details to aid future research in this topic.\footnote{Library \librarylink, HTML documentation \doclink, replication code \explink. \section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Systematic Disagreement Detection}
Prior work  on analyzing annotations in subjective tasks has shown that \ac{sysdis} may be caused by different points-of-view between groups \citep{davani-etal-2022-dealing, uma2021, goyal2022your}. While current research mostly rests on using \ac{iaa} metrics between groups of a single annotator \ac{sdd}~\citep{davani-etal-2022-dealing, leonardelli-etal-2023-semeval, uma_2022_scaling, ivey-etal-2025-nutmeg, petra_2022_handling, van_der_Velden}, these metrics suffer from severe limitations, which have motivated the development of new approaches.

\paragraph{Limitations of Inter-Annotator Agreement} \ac{iaa} metrics such as Cohen's kappa, Krippendorff's alpha, and Fleiss's kappa aim to distinguish \ac{sysdis} disagreement from general disagreement through statistical chance-correction. However, these metrics are often difficult to apply to intergroup \ac{iaa} without extensions or restrictive assumptions (e.g., no missing labels or equal numbers of annotations). They have also been criticized for failing to account for differences arising from social, cultural, and demographic backgrounds, producing results that are difficult to compare across studies \citep{Feinstein1990, Byrt1993, van_der_Velden, checco_2017}, being sensitive to class imbalance~\citep{Feinstein1990}, and relying on questionable assumptions about chance agreement~\citep{checco_2017}. While recent work has addressed some of these limitations with methods that improve applicability and robustness \citep{van_der_Velden, wong-etal-2021-cross, ivey-etal-2025-nutmeg}, they are much more complex and less intuitive, indicating that \ac{iaa} may not be the correct tool for analysis of minority viewpoints.

\paragraph{Alternatives} A different approach \citep{akhtar_2019} utilizes $\chi^2$ to estimate in-group vs out-group variance. Others frame \ac{sysdis} detection as a cluster identification problem, applying either parametric (\ac{gmm}) \citep{checco_2017}, or non-parametric~\citep{pavlopoulos-likas-2024, Mignemi2024} approaches to the annotation histograms. For our work, we use a non-parametric disagreement measure, \ac{ndfu}, which has been shown to correlate better with human judgment of disagreement than established measures \citep{pavlopoulos-likas-2024}, as we also verify in Fig.~\ref{fig:measures}. \ac{ndfu} (Eq.~\ref{eq:ndfu}) takes values in the range $[0,1]$, where values close to $0$ indicate no polarization (i.e., opinions are concentrated around a single mode). Intuitively, \ac{ndfu} measures how prominent the maximum secondary peak is compared to the main peak. 
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:ndfu}
	\mathrm{nDFU}(X) =
	\frac{
		\max_{i \neq m}
		\left(	
		f_i -
		\begin{cases}
			f_{i-1}, & \text{if } i > m \\
			f_{i+1}, & \text{if } i < m
		\end{cases}
		\right)
	}{f_m},
	m = \arg\max_i f_i
\end{equation}
\noindent where, $X$ denotes the set of annotations, $f_i$ the relative frequency of the $i$-th annotation (e.g., the frequency of \texttt{2} in a scale of 1--5), and $m$ the histogram peak.

\paragraph{What About Attribution?} \ac{iaa} analysis may be useful for exploration and identification of some controversial items, but fails to explain the reason \emph{why} they are controversial \citep{abercrombie-etal-2025-consistency, vitsakis-etal-2023-ilab}. A purpose-designed method for the identification of \ac{atsysdis} is the \ac{au} method \citep{pavlopoulos-likas-2024}, which builds upon \ac{ndfu}. It states that if a single item's annotations are multimodal (contains \ac{sysdis}), but unimodal for all annotator subgroups in a specific \ac{sdd} (contains disagreement but not \ac{sysdis}), then polarization is explained by those groups (\ac{atsysdis}). Though promising, this mechanism operates on the assumption that \emph{all} polarization can be explained by a single \ac{sdd}, which we prove to be impossible in many realistic cases (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).


\subsection{Using LLMs for Subjective Annotation}

\paragraph{Persona Prompting} To address subjectivity in annotation, several \ac{nlp} approaches incorporate annotator metadata into LLM annotation \citep{davani-etal-2023-hate, rottger-etal-2022-two, fleisig-etal-2023-majority, gupta_2023_same}. A significant limitation is the requirement for annotator metadata, which can be side-stepped through embedding clustering \citep{vitsakis-etal-2024-voices}, at greater computational cost and harder evaluation. Our approach instead directly clusters the annotations, which we believe provides a comparatively simpler and more interpretable mechanism.

\paragraph{Persona Engineering} At the time of writing, there is no established taxonomy of persona prompting techniques. Generally, personas may be authored by hand \citep{zhang-etal-2018-personalizing, mazare-etal-2018-training}, generated using observed annotator attributes \citep{argyle2023_out}, composed of answers to extensive questionnaires \citep{park2026llmagentsgroundedselfreports},  or recently, generated entirely using LLMs \citep{taubenfeld-etal-2024-systematic}.
The prompt itself may be structured \citep{yang-etal-2025-crafting, wang-etal-2025-know, Park2024CharacterGPTAP} or described through natural language \citep{zhang-etal-2018-personalizing, li-etal-2025-far, taubenfeld-etal-2024-systematic}. The effects of these methods on LLMs have not yet been studied and are largely outside the scope of this work. Since our goal is to explore disagreement patterns across LLMs and humans (RQ~\ref{rq:llm}), we select attributes from the human annotators of each dataset and format them using JSON instead of natural language, in order to facilitate comparisons across experimental conditions.

\paragraph{Persona Reliability} Prior work suggests that LLMs are not necessarily reliable annotators when prompted with personas, as they tend to reproduce stereotypes \citep{lutz-etal-2025-prompt, liu-etal-2024-evaluating-large} and perform unreliably for minority groups \citep{lutz-etal-2025-prompt, kapania-etal-2025-examining}. Additionally, some evidence suggests that task-specific improvements may be not be replicable outside of the datasets on which models are trained \citep{lee-etal-2023-large}. Our work ultimately supports the view that persona prompting is not reliable by itself in all tasks and must be separately evaluated when used.

Finding appropriate subjective datasets to test LLMs on is difficult for many reasons:
\begin{enumerate*}
	\item very few include annotator metadata \citep{vitsakis-etal-2024-voices},
	\item most include only three annotators per item (e.g., \shortcite{mulki-etal-2019-l, das2024investigatingannotatorbiaslarge, tonneau-etal-2024-languages, kumar2021tweetblmhatespeechdataset, tonneau-etal-2025-hateday}),
	\item many datasets are sampled from the same source, negatively impacting the breadth of possible generalizable findings \citep{vidgen_2021_directions}, and
	\item annotation guidelines are frequently not included \citep{vidgen_2021_directions}, complicating replication.
\end{enumerate*} 
Additionally, meta-studies on subjective datasets \citep{Poletto2021, vidgen_2021_directions} do not give special importance to the sparsity of annotations per item, which is an important consideration.\footnote{\S\ref{sec:discussion} shows that the real bottleneck in many \ac{nlp} datasets is not the amount of recorded \acp{sdd} nor the amount of items, but rather the amount of information (number of annotators) per item.}

We use five datasets from current \ac{nlp} research that contain \ac{sdd} information at the level of individual annotators:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The \textit{Kumar} dataset \citep{kumar-et-al-2021} which contains annotations for Toxicity detection.
	\item The \textit{Sap} ``Breadth of Posts'' dataset with the ``\textit{toyou}'' labels \citep{sap-etal-2022-annotators} for \ac{hs} detection.
	\item The \textit{DICES-350} (\textit{Q3\_bias\_targeting\_inherited\_attributes} label) and \textit{ DICES-990} (\textit{Q3\_bias\_incites\_hatred} label) datasets \citep{dices}, which pertain to LLM response safety. Our choice of labels allows us to use them as \ac{hs} datasets, capturing conceptually similar \acp{sdd} despite the lack of shared labels between them. There is little-to-not information around these labels, since neither a description nor annotation guidelines are provided.
	\item The POPQUORN \citep{pei-jurgens-2023-annotator} dataset using the \textit{``offensiveness''} label for \ac{hs} detection.\footnote{While ``politeness'' labels are included, they may not directly translate to toxicity; comments can simultaneously be toxic and phrased politely. Indeed, toxicity is not mentioned in the methodology of the original POPQUORN dataset and is considered a separate \ac{nlp} task in other work \citep{ziems2024can, lukito-etal-2024-comparing}.}
\end{itemize}

A brief overview of the dataset characteristics is provided in Table \ref{tab:datasets}. The Kumar dataset is notable for its large scale, featuring over 100,000 annotated items and 10 distinct \acp{sdd}.\footnote{In order to allow for a full comparison between human and LLM annotations, we use a sample of 1{,}000 items from the Kumar dataset (\S\ref{sec:llms}).} In contrast, the DICES datasets have fewer annotated items but involve more than an order of magnitude more annotators per item than the others. An exploratory analysis of these datasets is available in App.~\ref{app:ann_details}. Note that not all datasets can be used for all stages of our study; the DICES datasets do not include enough reproducibility information to re-annotate using LLMs, while the Kumar dataset does not include enough human annotations for any methodology to find \ac{atsysdis} (Table~\ref{tab:real-data-methods}).


\section{Empirically Testing Proposed Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{On Real Data}

\input{generated/real_data_comparison.tex}

We first test prior methods of attributing \ac{atsysdis} to our real-life, human-annotated datasets. Through this comparison we aim to both explore the datasets themselves by finding \acp{sdd} on which multiple methods attribute \ac{atsysdis} and as a sanity-check on the methods themselves.

\paragraph{Methods Considered} We compare the effectiveness of our method (introduced in \S\ref{sec:apunim}), the \ac{au} framework proposed by \citet{pavlopoulos-likas-2024}, the $\chi^2$ method presented in \citet{akhtar_2019}, \ac{gmm} clustering used in \citet{checco_2017}, and a baseline using \ac{iaa} ($\Delta \alpha$ Krippendorff) on simulated distributions. In this case, the \ac{gmm} method is not run, as it requires the exact same annotators to annotate the items in each dataset, which is not the case for any of our datasets.\footnote{Regardless of empirical results, this limitation makes the \ac{gmm} method extremely restrictive in realistic annotation settings.}

\paragraph{Results} Table~\ref{tab:real-data-methods} shows whether each of the methods attributes \ac{atsysdis} and on which \acp{sdd}. We observe that:
\begin{enumerate*}
	\item Two of the proposed methodologies do not yield any meaningful results. The \ac{au} method fails to find any \ac{atsysdis} attribution pattern in \emph{any} of the \acp{sdd} in any of the datasets, while the $\chi^2$ method reports that \ac{atsysdis} can be attributed \emph{all-but-one} \ac{sdd}.
	
	\item In contrast, both \ac{iaa} and our own method report some shared findings across the DICES-350, DICES-990 and Sap datasets, while neither reports any attributable \acp{sdd} in Kumar. In fact, we observe that attributable \acp{sdd} (namely \textit{Education, Race, Gender}) are commonly shared between datasets.
	
	\item Unlike \ac{iaa}, own method finds that the POPQUORN dataset does have \acp{sdd} on which \ac{atsysdis} can be attributed.
\end{enumerate*} 
The fact that \emph{three of the four currently proposed \ac{atsysdis} attribution metrics are not usable} in any of our datasets is troubling and requires investigation.


\subsection{Diagnosing Proposed Methods With Synthetic Data}

\begin{figure}[t]

\paragraph{Generating \ac{atsysdis}} In both real-life human and LLM annotation we lack a ground truth for \ac{atsysdis}. This limitation can be circumvented by constructing annotation distributions by hand, since we can know which \ac{sdd} explains \ac{atsysdis} and can test whether it is correctly attributed by each method. Additionally, by constructing these distributions we can test these methods in specific annotation scenarios reflected by our choice of parameters. We define an annotation $y_{ij}$ by annotator $i$ for item $j$ as $y_{ij} = b_j + g_i p_j + \epsilon_{ij}$, where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $b_j$  represents a baseline rating around which all annotations for an item $j$  are clustered  (e.g., $b_j=5$ for an obviously toxic comment on a toxicity scale of 1--5).
	
	\item $g_i \sim \text{Uniform}(0, \frac{1}{2})$ denotes group identity. We use sampling to represent each annotator internalizing group identity differently.
	
	\item $p_j$ represents real-life group polarization. We use two different formulations:
	\begin{enumerate*}
		\item \textbf{Unidirectional}: $p_j \sim \text{Uniform}(0, \delta)$. The group always favors higher ratings across all items, though its magnitude varies per item.
		
		\item \textbf{Bidirectional}: $p_j \sim \text{Uniform}(-\delta, +\delta)$. Annotators of the same group use systematically higher ratings in some items and lower in others. See Fig.~\ref{fig:ndfu_discussion} for an example.
	\end{enumerate*}
	
	\item $\epsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, 1.8)$ represents general disagreement. 
\end{itemize}
We use bootstrapping to determine whether a score is statistically significant and counts as a detection. For each experiment, we create $200$ items across $40$ independent runs, used to calculate standard error. Our hypothetical \ac{sdd} features two groups, although ablations for $k=\{3,5,10\}$ groups per \ac{sdd} are shown in Fig~\ref{fig:measures-multiple} in App.~\ref{app:ablation}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to per-Item Annotation Counts} Fig.~\ref{fig:measures} shows the performance of each method on our synthetic annotations. \ac{iaa} (orange squares) performs exceptionally in the case of eighty annotators per item even when differences in opinion (y-axis) are small (top-left). \ac{iaa} however requires a larger difference in opinion to detect \ac{atsysdis} when twenty annotators are available (middle-left) and collapses when only six annotators are available per item (bottom left). \ac{iaa} is also sensitive to the size of the compared groups; detection performance degrades significantly when we compare a minority group (16 vs. 64 annotators; plots on the right). On the other hand, the \ac{au} method (blue diamonds) only detects polarization when eighty annotators per item are available and differences in opinion are quantitatively large (top-left), but is resistant to minority group scenarios (top-right). This result is expected; \ac{au} utilizes clustering and is therefore largely invariant to the sizes of the opinion clusters. However, its absolute definition of \ac{atsysdis} attribution (\S\ref{sec:related}) renders it sensitive to noisy opinion histograms, which are common when few annotators per item exist.

This is true both for means-effects models, and \ac{ndfu} comparisons between groups.}

\paragraph{Inter-Item Annotation Aggregation} The $\chi^2$ and \ac{gmm} methods (green upright and yellow reverse triangles respectively) generally fail at attributing \ac{atsysdis} to any group in the bidirectional polarization scenario (solid lines). However, when defining polarization unidirectionally (dashed, faded lines), they perform exceptionally. This observation suggests that these metrics assume that across items, ratings of group A have a different average than group B. This assumption is valid for the unidirectional polarization scenario, but not the bidirectional one.\footnote{We control for our assumption of a non-fixed $g_i$ group polarization effect via the ablation shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:measures-fixed} in App.~\ref{app:ablation}.} Specifically, $\chi^2$ pools all annotations into a single contingency table, while \ac{gmm} clusters annotators by their mean rating across items. Fig.~\ref{fig:ndfu_discussion} shows a synthetic scenario where both approaches would fail, as bidirectional polarization effects cancel out in the aggregate, making the two groups appear statistically indistinguishable.

\paragraph{Results} Overall, our results suggest that \emph{current metrics for \ac{atsysdis} attribution are sensitive to one or more conditions that may occur in realistic annotation setups} for subjective \ac{nlp} datasets. \ac{iaa} is sensitive to minority groups, which are the subject of many content moderation tasks, but are not evenly represented in most datasets (see Tables~\ref{tab:dices-stats},\ref{tab:sap-stats},\ref{tab:kumar-stats} in App.~\ref{app:ann_details}) and on low annotator counts per item (also common, see Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). The latter is also required by \ac{au}.

\begin{finding}
	\label{finding:methods}
	Current methodologies revealing \ac{atsysdis} generally fail in realistic annotation conditions. One of the reasons is that disagreements may conflict across items, which may compromise cross-item annotation aggregation.
\end{finding}


\section{Comparing Human and LLM Polarization}

\subsection{Quantifying Attribution to Annotator Dimensions}

We estimate \ac{sysdis} using \ac{ndfu} (see Eq.~\ref{eq:apunim}). Specifically, we measure the \ac{sysdis} of each group $g$ for an item $c$ as $P_g(c) = \textit{nDFU}(A_g(c))$, where $A_g(c)$ is the set of annotations for item $c$ created by annotators belonging to a group $g$. We compare this quantity to the ``apriori'' polarization present in the item $c$, which is defined as the \emph{expected} (average) unimodality of theoretical groups $\mathrm{P}_{\textit{apr}}(c) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{1}^{k}  \mathrm{nDFU}(A_{r}(c))$, where $A_{r}$ is a set selected from a random partition of the original annotation set and $k$ is a hyperparameter (higher $k$ yields more accurate estimation, at a greater computational cost). Intuitively, a high $\mathrm{P}_{\textit{apr}}(c)$ score means that much of the \ac{sysdis} is likely not driven by any single group.

Since the  $\mathrm{P}_{\textit{apr}}(c)$ and $P_g(c)$ quantities represent very noisy estimates due to the lack of annotators per item, a direct comparison would not be reliable. We therefore exploit the Law of Large Numbers to compute the statistic on the dataset level and average both quantities for all items in the dataset $D$. Normalizing this difference yields the measure described in Eq.~\ref{eq:ndfu}, which we call \emph{apunim}:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:apunim}
	\textit{apunim}(D, g) = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{c \in D} \left[ \mathrm{P}_{\textit{apr}}(c) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{c \in D} \left[ P_g(c)\right] }{1 - \mathbb{E}_{c \in D} \left[ \mathrm{P}_{\textit{apr}}(c) \right]}
\end{equation}

The \emph{sign} of apunim carries information agreement-based attribution does not:
\begin{itemize}
	\item$\textit{apunim}(g) \approx 0$: The group $g$ does not meaningfully disagree with the rest of the annotators.
	\item $\textit{apunim}(g) > 0$: The group $g$ has fundamental disagreements with the rest of the annotators. 
	\item $\textit{apunim}(g) < 0$: The annotators of group $g$ are polarized between themselves.
\end{itemize}

Apunim thus measures the degree to which  \ac{atsysdis} can be attributed to an annotator group in a set of items. As demonstrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:measures}, our measure is robust to few annotators per item and imbalanced (minority) group sizes. Furthermore, unlike some prior methods \citep{akhtar_2019, checco_2017}, apunim allows us to attribute \ac{atsysdis} to a specific group, not just a \ac{sdd} (e.g., only ``men'' instead of ``gender'' as a whole), allowing fine-grained analysis. We also include a perturbation-based p-value estimation which we describe in App.~\ref{app:apunim}, which also contains a discussion of theoretical design decisions, limitations and computational details.

\subsection{What Does This Mean for LLM Annotation?} 

Our results may indicate that LLMs do not intrinsically capture the differences in opinion between human groups, or miscalculate the degree that each \ac{sdd} characteristic influences human opinion. Our study thus joins a growing number of  work questioning the capabilities of persona-prompting \citep{lutz-etal-2025-prompt, liu-etal-2024-evaluating-large} and the limitations of LLMs in creating data for subjective social science tasks \citep{rossi_2024, wu2026llmbasedsocialsimulationsrequire, kapania-etal-2025-examining}. While persona-prompting meaningfully increases the variance (here, disagreement) of LLM annotations, as argued in prior work \citep{frohling-etal-2025-personas}, the direction of this variance is inconsistent.

This limitation would indicate the need for careful evaluation and calibration of LLM outputs on sensitive applications such as content moderation \citep{civelli-etal-2026-ideology} and facilitation \citep{korre-etal-2025-evaluation}, which so far assume that LLMs can be used as autonomous agents. It also may indicate that research focusing on improving annotation diversity through persona prompting \citep{frasheri2025persona, frohling-etal-2025-personas} may lead to meaningless or miscalibrated data when applied outside of specific annotation scenarios with limited scope. Finally, there is a general concern that LLMs training on biased LLM-generated data may amplify already-existing biases \citep{ren-etal-2024-bias, pmlr-v202-taori23a}, which is especially important with the possible proliferation of persona-prompted mass-annotated synthetic datasets amplifying miscalibrated points of view across model iterations.


\section{The Ideal Subjective Dataset}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Categorizing Attributes of Annotation Datasets}

As discussed in \S\ref{sec:datasets}, there is a severe lack of datasets containing annotator metadata for subjective tasks. Given that the creation of these datasets is difficult, expensive and time-consuming, it is important to acknowledge and prioritize certain properties that are necessary for analyzing subjectivity across annotator groups. Since there is to our knowledge no taxonomy on the shape of annotated datasets, we distinguish three main properties:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \ac{dc}: the number of \emph{items} in a dataset. Due to the focus on training and benchmarking datasets in \ac{nlp}, this property is usually the one with the most focus and importance \citep{rossi_2024}.
	
	\item \ac{gc}: the number of \emph{annotators} and their distribution across \acp{sdd}. A large annotator pool is required to get sufficient coverage on the examined groups and allows both higher fidelity of each \ac{sdd} and more such \acp{sdd} to be examined. To our knowledge, there are no studies explicitly discussing this property.
	
	\item \ac{ad}: the number of \emph{annotators per item}. This property is often neglected in practice, with many datasets \citep{mulki-etal-2019-l, das2024investigatingannotatorbiaslarge, tonneau-etal-2024-languages, kumar2021tweetblmhatespeechdataset, tonneau-etal-2025-hateday} assuming ``gold labels'' can be inferred by three annotators. Similarly to \ac{gc}, we can not find any discussion of this property, with some studies recommending more than three annotators but still defaulting to majority-voting \citep{Beck2023}.
\end{itemize}

While the field of subjective \ac{nlp} tasks has thankfully moved away from single-annotated items and majority voting, the lack of discussion on \ac{gc} and \ac{ad} suggests that the shift has been incremental rather than substantive. A clear signal can not be always obtained when only a few cursory \acp{sdd} are reported instead of a comprehensive list of both innate characteristics and personal opinions. \footnote{The Kumar dataset is an excellent example of a comprehensive subjective dataset (\S\ref{sec:datasets}.} Likewise, six annotators per item may not be adequate, even when a comprehensive list of \acp{sdd} is known, as we demonstrate in the next section.


\subsection{The Case for Annotation Density}

\paragraph{\ac{unpol}} Some theoretical models such as \ac{au} make the assumption that all \ac{atsysdis} is attributable to some annotator \ac{sdd}, which has not been empirically verified in real-world annotation settings. We define this phenomenon ``\acf{unpol}'' for a single item $c$ as the minimum \ac{ndfu} exhibited by any arbitrary dimension:\footnote{This dimension need not be known in the dataset, as we consider all possible dimensions in our sample, both known and latent. This formulation also covers all possible combinations of such groups.} $\mathrm{Pol}_{inh}(c) = \min_{S \in G_c} \left\{ \mathrm{nDFU}(S) \right\}$, where $G_c$ is the collection of all subsets $S$ of annotations for an item $c$ such that $\lvert S \rvert \ge 3$ and $S_j$ is a random sample drawn from $G_c$ for $j=1, \dots, k$. Since \ac{ndfu} operates only when a group has at least three annotators (Eq.~\ref{eq:ndfu}), this yields $\sum_{k=3}^{n} \binom{n}{k}$ possible groups, which is computationally intractable for large annotator counts. We can therefore use Monte Carlo sampling by drawing $k$ random partitions with random sizes: $\mathrm{Pol}_{inh}^{MC}(c) = \min_{j=1}^{k} \left\{ \mathrm{nDFU}(S_j) \right\}$, which serves as an upper bound (as it considers a strict subset of $\mathrm{Pol}_{inh}(c)$).

\paragraph{Does It Exist?} We compute the \ac{unpol} for each item across the five datasets. For Sap and Kumar, we use the exhaustive formulation, whereas for the two DICES datasets we rely on the MC approximation with $k=1000$. We also that \ac{unpol} typically exists only on smaller annotation counts, as evidenced in Fig~\ref{fig:inherent}; we observe \ac{unpol} for the Sap/Kumar datasets (which are only annotated by six and five annotators respectively, see Table~\ref{tab:datasets}) and on POPQUORN only when it is subsampled down to six annotators per item. This pattern is also validated on both synthetic and LLM annotated data, as shown by Fig.~\ref{fig:inherent-synthetic-llm} in App.~\ref{app:unpol}.\footnote{See App.~\ref{app:unpol} for hypotheses on what creates \ac{unpol}. Also, examples of items with high \ac{unpol} can be found in Table~\ref{tab:inherent-examples} in App.~\ref{app:unpol}.}\footnote{It is unclear why the DICES datasets do not produce any \ac{unpol} even when repeatedly subsampled. We hypothesize than either $k$ is not large enough (which would make the computation prohibitively expensive), or the annotations themselves may not be as polarized. The latter hypothesis is plausible, as both DICES datasets do feature less polarized comments (see Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset-polarization} in App.~\ref{app:ann_details}) and also because their text targets (LLM responses) may not be as ambiguous as human comments.}
\begin{finding}
	\label{finding:inherent}
	Items may exhibit a degree of \ac{unpol} that cannot be attributed to any known or latent annotator grouping.
\end{finding}


\subsection{How Should We Design Subjective Datasets?}
While \ac{dc} may be convenient for training classifiers, relying on it without rigorous evaluation of \ac{sysdis} can lead to misconfigured or biased models \citep{sap-etal-2019-risk}, evaluation which is impossible without the necessary \ac{dc}). However, it comes at the expense of the other two (arguably more important) qualities. Generally, the \emph{easiest-to-use resources for training LLMs and classifiers may be the riskiest}, as meaningful, \ac{sysdis} cannot be reliably quantified (see the Kumar dataset in this study). We therefore recommend prioritizing dataset creation by first ensuring sufficient \ac{ad}, then achieving necessary \ac{gc} across underrepresented groups, and finally allowing budgetary constraints to determine \ac{dc}.

While this tension between the need for massive data volumes in AI training and the requirement of responsible social science to use deeply annotated, subjective datasets is unlikely to be resolved, a practical step is providing full annotation guidelines in both cases. If the annotation target and definitions are unknown, comparing results across different datasets becomes extremely difficult. As shown in \S\ref{sec:llms}, without clear guidelines, differences in polarization patterns remain obscured when analyzing data from multiple studies.


\section*{Conclusion}
In this study, we showed that current disagreement attribution measures tend to underperform in realistic annotation settings through a large scale empirical study. We designed a new measure which quantifies attribution of \acf{sysdis} to annotator \acp{sdd}, taking into account the limitations of real-life annotation datasets. Using this measure, we identified shared polarization patterns among gender and ethnicity groups across five subjective \ac{nlp} datasets. Additionally, we found that LLM annotators can create polarized annotations, but do not reproduce human polarization patterns, even when using prompt engineering. Our research is limited due to the information present in the datasets; namely, we discovered that due to low annotation counts per item, some polarization cannot be attributed to any combination of theoretical dimensions (``\acf{unpol}''). Overall, our work warns against using LLM persona prompting without rigorous, task-specific evaluation, and motivates the development of ``wider'' datasets with fewer but better annotated items.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
\paragraph{Dual Use Concerns} The primary objective of this study is to identify systemic sources of \ac{atsysdis} in subjective \ac{nlp} datasets to facilitate the construction of more fair and reliable AI systems. However, given that our methodology allows for the quantitative attribution of specific annotation patterns to \acp{sdd}, we acknowledge three primary risks inherent in this research.
\begin{enumerate*}
	\item \emph{Discriminatory Profiling}: If the measure is applied outside of a dataset quality analysis, its ability to correlate specific opinion patterns with annotator groups could be misused for surveillance or unjust profiling.
	\item \emph{Automated Bias Amplification}: The diagnostic patterns of disagreement we detect are reflections of human systemic biases. Without human oversight, training subsequent models on these patterns risks automating and entrenching existing societal prejudices.

\end{enumerate*}
We stress that our measure functions strictly as a diagnostic warning signal. Any deployment in a real-world system must mandate a human-in-the-loop review, ensuring that identified patterns lead to systemic improvements (e.g., policy reform, demographic diversity initiatives) rather than merely justifying an automated system. In any case, despite the risks, we believe that the benefits of this research outweigh the risks outlined above.

\section{Apunim Details}
\label{app:apunim}

\subsection{Design Decisions}

\begin{figure}
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ndfu_combined.png}
	\caption{Simulated experiment describing a polarizing comment where men and women agree between themselves, but disagree with the opposite gender.}
	\label{fig:ndfu-combined}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Defining Apriori Polarization} In \S\ref{sec:apunim}, we explained how our methodology needs to compare the polarization between the set of all annotations and those made by a specific group $g$. Direct comparisons are not advisable, since we would be comparing statistics between a set and its subset---in this case, standard practice dictates comparing the subset with its complement; i.e., the in-group vs. the out-group instead of the in-group vs. all the annotations. However, this also would not work; if we apply the subset-vs-complement method in the example of Fig.~\ref{fig:ndfu-combined}, we would end up subtracting the \acp{ndfu} of men and women, which are similar as they are both unimodal distributions, falsely concluding that there is no polarization present.

\paragraph{Filtering Ineligible items} Because \textit{apunim} is fundamentally designed to quantify polarization, we exclude items that exhibit no measurable polarization ($nDFU(c)=0$). Furthermore, we also exclude items annotated exclusively by a single group (i.e., items where there are no two groups with at least three annotators each), since we require valid histograms for the non-parametric estimation of \ac{ndfu} (see Eq.~\ref{eq:ndfu}). This is an unfortunately common scenario, since many \ac{nlp} datasets feature only $5-6$ annotations per item. In a best-case scenario of 5 annotators split evenly between two groups, we would get a 3-2 split.


\subsection{Statistical Estimation}

Since annotation histograms may be noisy, we additionally assess whether the observed apunim value could arise by chance. To assess statistical significance, we construct a null distribution of apunim values from the randomized partitions and compare the observed apunim against this distribution using a one-sample Student-\(t\) test. Thus, the null hypothesis states that the observed polarization is indistinguishable from polarization induced by random annotator assignments:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:pvalue}
	\begin{split}
		&H_0:\ 
		\bar{\textit{apunim}}_{\mathrm{rand}}(d,g)
		=
		\textit{apunim}(d,g) \\
		&H_a:\ 
		\bar{\textit{apunim}}_{\mathrm{rand}}(d,g)
		\neq
		\textit{apunim}(d,g)
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
\noindent where \(\bar{\textit{apunim}}_{\mathrm{rand}}(d,g)\) denotes the average apunim values obtained from $t$ randomized annotator partitions.

We account for the presence of multiple subgroups in each \ac{sdd} using a multiple-hypothesis correction. Since our test is parametric (App.~\ref{app:pvalue}), it assumes that there exist (1) enough data-points to reliably run the means-test, (2) enough annotations per \ac{sdd} group to estimate \acp{ndfu}, (3) the apunim values of the random partitions are independent and identically distributed. Condition (1) can be safely assumed for most datasets, because they feature enough items to reliably assume normal residuals (see \S\ref{sec:datasets}). We discuss condition (2) in \S\ref{sec:discussion}. Condition (3) is partly fulfilled; the partitions are identically distributed, but may be dependent on the sample distribution. 

Replacing the parametric means test with a non-parametric alternative would relax assumptions about the underlying distribution but would not resolve the potential dependence between the random apunims. We initially attempted to implement a permutation test on the empirical (random apunim) distribution, but this resulted in excessive computational costs.

While the test operates on a single group in principle, in practice we often want to test polarization for all subgroups of a specific \ac{sdd}. Since we are simultaneously considering multiple hypotheses, we apply a multiple comparison correction to the resulting p-values---specifically the Holm method~\citep{holms}. The test is parameterized by the confidence level of our test, which is used to tune the strength of the correction; we can increase this value to make our test more conservative towards multiple hypotheses~\citep{ChenFengYi2017}).


\subsection{Software}

\paragraph{Installation and Usage}
We release the \texttt{apunim} package, which is a Python library that implements both the \ac{ndfu} and apunim calculations, as well as its statistical test. The software is available on PyPi We also provide high-level documentation in HTML format that includes an introduction, guide, and full low-level documentation for each of the two provided functions (\doclink).

We use version \texttt{1.0.6} of the \texttt{apunim} library for our experiments. We use 100 random iterations for the apriori value calculation and the p-value estimation (App.~\ref{app:pvalue}), and set a confidence level of $\alpha=0.05$. The relative library setup is provided as a \texttt{conda} environment file to ensure reproducibility. The code is open-source, licensed under the \ac{gpl} and can be found on the project's repository (\explink). The library is cross-platform, works for any Python 3 version, and requires very few dependencies (namely \texttt{numpy, scipy, statsmodels}).

\paragraph{Implementation Details}
By default, the computation of the apunim values happens on a single thread. In cases of serious computational cost, we recommend running the apunim calculation on multiple concurrent processes, since all operations are CPU-bound and thread-safe. This may be useful in cases where there are multiple \acp{sdd}, each of which is computationally expensive. In order to lower the computational cost required, we re-use the random partitions, which are originally calculated to obtain the apriori polarization values, for the p-value calculations (App.~\ref{app:pvalue}). This means that in our current implementation, the number of groups for estimating apriori polarization, and the number of apunim values used in the p-value calculation have to be the same.

While the library efficiently handles large numbers of annotators through vectorized operations, performance degrades significantly when processing a high volume of items, particularly when annotators belong to multiple distinct groups. For instance, while experiments across all datasets in \S\ref{sec:datasets} complete within a minute, the Kumar dataset necessitates approximately one hour of computation using commercial hardware. Furthermore, the annotator variance analysis detailed in Fig.~\ref{fig:std_error} required over ten hours. While this latter time requirement is anticipated due to the inherently demanding nature and non-standard application of our library, it demonstrates the existence of a potential scaling bottleneck. The same applies to a lesser extent for the subsampling experiments on the DICES datasets, taking collectively three hours of computation.

We hypothesize that this bottleneck is likely caused by repeated creation of numpy arrays, resulting in repeated memory allocations for each subgroup, in each item, for each \ac{sdd}. We plan on profiling and addressing it in the next releases of our software. Until then, we suggest either using multiprocessing for each \ac{sdd} subgroup, or running the different experiments in parallel (indeed, we use the \texttt{gnu-parallel} package~\citep{tange_2025_16944306} for our own experiments). We also note that the Kumar dataset also requires substantial RAM resources, estimated around 42--46GB of dedicated RAM should the whole dataset be considered (this is not a requirement for loading the sample used in this study). The CPU-bound experiments require approximately 3 hours to run, assuming a computer capable of efficient multiprocessing.

Finally, we note that in Fig.~\ref{fig:std_error}, we removed values where there was a lack of items annotated by the specified number of annotators (e.g., only 7\% of items in DICES-990 were annotated by more than 73 annotators---see App.~\ref{app:ann_details}).


\section{Exploring \ac{unpol}}
\label{app:unpol}

\paragraph{Why Does it Exist?}
Unlike disagreement, \ac{unpol} does not provide any information, but rather represents noise or uncertainty translated in terms of polarization. In order to minimize it, we need to find its cause. Since the DICES datasets are the only ones without \emph{any} items with \ac{unpol} leads us to two hypotheses:

\begin{hypothesis}[Low annotator count]
	\label{hyp:few-annotators}
	A low number of annotators per item may provide our model with insufficient information.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{hypothesis}[Inaccessible groups]
	\label{hyp:groups}
	\ac{unpol} may arise from the presence of partially represented groups within the annotator pool which may go undetected if they are represented by only one or two annotators, as \ac{ndfu} is undefined for these groups. 
\end{hypothesis}

Both of these explanations are plausible and reflect known limitations of most \ac{nlp} datasets. Unfortunately, we can not confirm any of these hypotheses.

In all cases, including human annotations (Fig.~\ref{fig:inherent}), low annotator counts per item seem to be the cause of \ac{sysdis}. Indeed, we observe \ac{unpol} from most models when repeatedly subsampling six annotators (Fig.~\ref{fig:inherent-llms}). In the case of the Sap and Kumar datasets, human annotators show significantly higher \ac{unpol} values, which is not the case for POPQUORN. Instruction prompts can influence the extent of \ac{unpol}, but inconsistently. Providing adversarial instruction prompts seems to increase \ac{unpol}, although the effect of each prompt seems to be dataset-dependent.

\paragraph{Do Fixed Group Effects Affect our Experiments?} In our synthetic
experiments, annotators within a group do not share the same opinion: each
annotator's group effect is sampled, with $g_i \sim U(-1/2, 0)$ for one
group and $g_i \sim U(0, 1/2)$ for the other. In the multigroup experiments,
each annotator in the polarizing group has a strength $h_i \sim U(0, 1)$.
To test whether our conclusions depend on this choice, we rerun both
experiments with a fixed group effect, where every annotator in a group
shifts by exactly the same amount ($g_i = \pm 1/2$ and $h_i = 1$). All other
settings, including the conditions, $\delta$ values, and number of
repetitions, are unchanged. Note that the fixed setting also doubles the
expected difference between groups at a given $\delta$, so it is both more
homogeneous and a stronger signal. Fig.~\ref{fig:measures-fixed} shows the
results.

\paragraph{How Does Dataset Size Influence Apunim?} Since our approach was designed for the small-scale datasets common in \ac{nlp}, we were cautious when testing it on the massive Kumar dataset. These groups however are largely not detected by apunim as significant either way (see Table~\ref{tab:apunim-main}).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Default Prompt} Since each of the datasets in \S\ref{sec:datasets} was annotated using different annotation guidelines and targets we use different instruction prompts for each dataset. The ``default'' annotation prompt is directly extracted from the studies in the case of Sap and Kumar, and inferred from the instruction guidelines used in the DICES datasets with minor modifications. We use standard persona prompting procedures sampled randomly from the available \acp{sdd} in each respective dataset. Twenty LLM annotators are selected for each item and model.

\paragraph{Ablation Prompts} The prompts used in the ablation study were provided by a GPT-5 and a local Gemma-4 (quantized) model by providing the original prompt and instructing it to produce four variants. The tested prompts use different methods to restrict the model's behavior. Besides the default prompt, we test four prompts that make the persona more central to the rating. All four ask the same question about the comment as the default prompt and append the persona description after the comment. They differ only in how they instruct the model to use the persona. \textit{Persona-focused} asks the model to rate the comment as its persona would personally perceive it, based on the persona's characteristics, attitudes, experiences, and sensitivities. \textit{Direct} states that the persona's characteristics are a mandatory prerequisite to consider and that the model must evaluate the comment exclusively as the persona would classify it. \textit{Single} asks the model to first identify the single most influential trait of its persona, and then to derive the rating by matching the comment to that trait alone. \textit{Stereotype} asks the model to be very sensitive to its persona's characteristics and to give substantial weight to the persona's sensitivities, values, experiences, and likely reactions, rather than to the judgment of a generic or average person. The full prompts are in App.~\ref{app:prompts}.

\paragraph{Hardware and Execution Times} We use a single Quadro RTX 6000 GPU for tasks requiring small (7-8B) models and two such GPUs for the rest. The annotation task, including ablations, requires approximately 300 hours of computation to complete. We use the \texttt{transformers} library to load and manage the LLMs. We release the relevant code in the supplementary material. Our codebase is platform-independent except for three high-level bash scripts and a full environment file is included for reproduction.

For the DICES-350 and DICES-990 harmfulness tasks, human annotations are heavily concentrated at the lowest harmfulness value, whereas most LLMs assign substantially more probability to moderate and high harmfulness values. We examine the following conditions for selecting models and datasets for our LLM annotation experiment. All ablation experiments use a sampled 20\% fraction of the full LLM annotation dataset used in the main text.

\paragraph{The LLMs should be consistent given the same prompt} While we have fixed all relevant seeds, it is possible that generation deviates across repeated model prompting given the same input \citep{bisbee_2023}. We check this condition by providing the model with the main annotation prompt (see App.~\ref{app:prompts}) five times and find that generation is indeed deterministic under our setup for all models (Table~\ref{tab:llm-consistency-repeats}).

\paragraph{The LLMs should be prompt insensitive} Small changes in the prompt have been shown to drastically affect LLM output in some tasks depending on the experimental setup \citep{razavi_sensitivity}. In order to verify whether any models are unreliable under our setup, we create two alternative formulations of each prompt without a change in substance using a GPT-5 model (free version), whose outputs we manually verified (see App.~\ref{app:prompts}). Table~\ref{tab:llm-consistency-variants} shows that while most LLMs are relatively robust to changes in formulation, the smaller ones (OLMo-7B, Qwen-7B and LLaMa-8B) are not. They are thus excluded from the annotation study.

\paragraph{The annotation guidelines should be unambiguous} The studies that provided the datasets used in our work (see \S\ref{sec:datasets}) do not include LLM annotation, nor equivalent LLM prompts, which meant we had to generate them from the human annotation guidelines ourselves. This was not a problem in the case of the Sap and Kumar datasets, since the annotation guidelines were short, simple and required minimal edits to be transformed into LLM annotation guidelines. However, the DICES datasets use poorly documented guidelines and the target label we used is an aggregation of multiple annotation tasks. For these reasons the prompt creation is non-trivial and prone to errors. Indeed, Table~\ref{tab:llm-consistency-cross-model} shows that inter-model agreement is extremely low for the DICES datasets, even when we remove the sensitive models (Table~\ref{tab:llm-consistency-cross-model-excluding}). Given that the DICES datasets are (1) problematic w.r.t. inherent polarization (see App.~\ref{app:unpol}), (2) conditioned on several dozen annotations per item (3) difficult to find reliable annotation prompts for LLMs, we decided to exclude them from the annotation study.
